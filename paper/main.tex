\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

Test-time training (TTT) lets a language model update part of its weights while it reads, but existing designs discard that adaptation at every document boundary. This thesis asks whether the adapted fast weights can be carried into the next document, how much that carry contributes (Q1), and which domains benefit most (Q2). The main finding is that a trained, gated fast-weight carry transfers across documents according to their relatedness. On 200 Wikipedia articles, a carry built from an article that the target mentions lowers the target's perplexity by 0.085, one built from an unrelated article by 0.051, and one built from text of another source raises it by 0.067, with every step of this gradient significant and replicated with a second training seed. Carried through a long chain of other documents of the same source, the state instead raises perplexity slightly, by 0.020 and 0.031 at the two seeds. Against dynamic evaluation tuned for the same transfer, the carry transfers about 0.3 percent more to related and to unrelated articles of the same source, using forward passes only, while dynamic evaluation adapts more strongly to a document's own text. The carry does not accumulate. Re-reading a document ten times helps no more than reading it twice.

We reach this design after diagnosing why a naive carry fails. We add In-Place TTT layers to the MLP down-projections of Qwen3-0.6B, train them with a Hebbian and a chunked delta update rule, and compare them against a LoRA-only model trained with identical data, schedule and learning rate, using paired evaluation regimes on 106 held-out documents from six domains. A naive carry contributes nothing measurable across documents, for both rules and at every checkpoint, with intervals of about $\pm 0.01$ on 24 documents. The read-time norm clip that keeps the fast weights stable is active on every measurement by a factor of at least 255, so only the direction of the carried state reaches the output, and removing the clip makes training collapse or switches the fast weights off. The redesign gives the carry its own clipped path with its own gate and a zero-initialised scale, trains it through the boundary between slices, and starts the main gate nearly closed. Its clip stays saturated, so it works by training which direction is carried, not by relieving the clip. A single-seed ablation at step 200 suggests that the first two changes work together, each giving a smaller significant gain alone.

Within a document, the redesigned carry lowers perplexity on later slices by 0.06 to 0.14 through 600 training steps, against $-0.002$ to 0.014 for the original Hebbian design and 0.007 to 0.027 for the original delta rule, and the model has the largest lead over the matched LoRA baseline in the study, 0.234 perplexity against 0.203 for the delta rule, with overlapping intervals. Placing the previous text directly in attention is far stronger, and the carry recovers about four percent of its gain, so its use is where attention cannot reach. Most of a TTT model's lead over the matched baseline is the extra trainable capacity of the down-projection. For Q2, the gains differ across domains, and over six domains they show no clear relation to how hard a domain is for the base model.

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch-intro}

\section{Background}

A trained large language model answers every input with the same parameters. This makes inference efficient, but it also means the model cannot specialise to the context in which it is being used. In-context learning lets a model adapt to its input without changing any weight, but only while that input stays in its context window \citep{brown2020language,dong2024survey}. Test-time training (TTT) relaxes this restriction by updating part of the model on the input it is currently reading, using a self-supervised objective that needs no labels \citep{sun2020ttt}. Applied to language models, TTT has been used to build sequence layers whose hidden state is itself a small model trained online \citep{sun2025learning}, long-term memory modules placed beside attention \citep{behrouz2025titans}, and most recently In-Place TTT \citep{feng2026inplace}, which turns the MLP down-projection of an existing transformer into a fast weight that adapts while the model reads. These methods descend from fast weights, weights that change during inference and act as an associative memory of what has been read \citep{ba2016using,schlag2021linear}.

\section{Research problem and gap}

Every one of these methods resets its adapted state at the end of each document. In-Place TTT does so deliberately, to prevent information leaking between unrelated inputs, and never tests carrying the fast weights forward \citep{feng2026inplace}. Related work approaches persistence from other directions. Some methods meta-learn a test-time initialisation or update weights over one long context \citep{tandon2025endtoend,bansal2025letsnot,wang2026learning}, and others carry cached activations or memory tokens across segments of a single stream \citep{dai2019transformerxl,rae2019compressive,bulatov2022rmt}. Dynamic evaluation adapts a model along its test sequence, and has been applied to successive texts, but by gradient steps on the slow weights \citep{krause2018dynamic,lazaridou2021mind}. None carries a feed-forward fast weight across independently bounded documents. If such a carry worked, a model could keep improving on a user, a codebase or a domain without any gradient update to its slow weights, which is a form of continual learning at inference time, a setting that surveys of lifelong learning for language models, organised around training-time updates, do not cover \citep{zheng2024towards}.

\section{Research questions}

This thesis addresses two research questions, as stated in the research proposal.
\begin{description}
  \item[Q1.] How much does carry, the information retained from the previous session, contribute to language model improvement?
  \item[Q2.] What kind of domains perform best with session-persistent TTT?
\end{description}

\section{Objective}

The primary objective is to develop and empirically analyse a session-persistent test-time training method for pretrained language models. Following the proposal, the method exposes two logically independent components that can be measured separately, within-document adaptation without any persistence, and cross-document persistence of the fast weight.

\section{Contributions}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The finding that a trained, gated fast-weight carry transfers across documents according to their relatedness. Its benefit falls steadily with the relatedness of the earlier text, from a related article to an unrelated one of the same source, and turns to harm for text from another source and after a long chain of other documents. It transfers more than dynamic evaluation of a LoRA model tuned for the same purpose, with forward passes only, it does not accumulate with repeated reading, and its main results replicate with a second training seed (Chapter~\ref{ch-design}).
  \item A carry design that makes this possible. A separate, zero-initialised carry path, trained through the boundary between slices, gives the largest lead over the matched baseline in the study, and a single-seed ablation suggests that its two main ingredients work together (Chapter~\ref{ch-design}).
  \item A diagnosis of why a naive carry fails. The read-time norm clip that stabilises the fast weights is saturated on every measurement, so an untrained carry reaches the output only as an uninformative direction, and in short probes training without the clip either collapses or switches the fast weights off (Chapter~\ref{ch-why}).
  \item An answer to Q1 for the naive carry. It contributes nothing measurable across documents, at three training checkpoints and for both update rules, against a reset at document boundaries in the style of In-Place TTT (Chapter~\ref{ch-q1}).
  \item An answer to Q2. The within-document and carry gains vary across six domains, and the variation shows no clear relation to domain difficulty (Chapter~\ref{ch-q2}).
  \item An evaluation protocol of paired regimes that isolates slow-weight training, fast-weight updates within a document, and state carried across slice and document boundaries, together with a LoRA-only control matched in data, schedule and learning rate, and a comparison with the preceding text in attention (Chapters~\ref{ch-eval} and~\ref{ch-design}).
  \item A session-persistent variant of In-Place TTT that carries the fast-weight state across documents with an exponential moving average, implemented for a Hebbian and a chunked delta update rule (Chapter~\ref{ch-method}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope}

As in the proposal, the study is scoped to inference-time improvement measured by perplexity on held-out text. It makes no claim that a model retains and can later use specific information, which would require behavioural testing. In the terms of \citet{song2026beyond}, our evidence is evidence of stream and domain adaptation, measured by perplexity. It does not show that a carried state stores information that the model can later retrieve. The proposal also planned individual RULER tasks \citep{hsieh2024ruler} as an additional held-out probe. They were left out. While the original designs carried nothing measurable across documents there was no carried signal for a retrieval probe to detect, and once the redesigned carry produced one, the remaining compute budget went to the relatedness, ablation, seed and dynamic-evaluation experiments that establish it in perplexity. Chapter~\ref{ch-limits} returns to this.

\section{Thesis outline}

Chapter~\ref{ch-lit} reviews the literature and positions the thesis against it. Chapter~\ref{ch-method} describes the method and Chapter~\ref{ch-eval} the experimental setup and evaluation protocol. Chapters~\ref{ch-q1} and~\ref{ch-q2} answer the two research questions, Chapter~\ref{ch-why} explains why the naive carry fails, and Chapter~\ref{ch-design} presents a redesigned carry whose transfer across documents follows their relatedness. Chapter~\ref{ch-discussion} relates the findings to the literature, Chapter~\ref{ch-limits} states the limitations and future work, and Chapter~\ref{ch-conclusion} concludes.

\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch-lit}

This chapter reviews the work that the thesis builds on and positions it against. It begins with test-time training and its relatives in test-time adaptation (Section~\ref{sec-lit-ttt}), then treats fast weights as associative memory (Section~\ref{sec-lit-fw}) and the update rules, gates and stabilisers that govern them (Section~\ref{sec-lit-rules}). It then reviews how earlier models keep information across segments and documents (Section~\ref{sec-lit-persist}), the wider literature on efficient and long-context transformers (Section~\ref{sec-lit-long}), continual learning and meta-learning (Section~\ref{sec-lit-cl}), dynamic evaluation (Section~\ref{sec-lit-dyneval}) and evaluation methodology (Section~\ref{sec-lit-eval}). It closes by positioning the thesis and stating the research gap (Section~\ref{sec-lit-gap}).

\section{Test-time training and test-time adaptation}
\label{sec-lit-ttt}

\subsection{Origins in vision}

Test-time training was introduced by \citet{sun2020ttt} for image classification under distribution shift. A network with a shared feature extractor and two heads, one for the main task and one for a self-supervised task such as rotation prediction, is trained jointly on both. At test time the shared extractor takes a few gradient steps on the self-supervised loss of the single test input, the prediction is made with the updated extractor, and the update is then discarded before the next input. The authors give a sufficient condition for this to help in the convex case, namely that the gradients of the self-supervised and main losses are positively correlated, and confirm the relation empirically on a deep network. They also propose an online variant that does not discard the update but starts each test input from the extractor adapted on the previous one. On a long stream of corrupted images its accuracy is still rising after tens of thousands of samples, but the variant assumes that the stream comes from one fixed or slowly changing distribution. On a genuine unknown shift, CIFAR-10.1, the authors report an improvement of 0.8 points and call it a small first step. Two features of this first paper recur throughout the field. The default is to reset after every input, and the one form of persistence it tests is persistence within a homogeneous stream.

Later work in vision kept the reset and changed the objective. \citet{gandelsman2022ttt} replace rotation prediction with masked-autoencoder reconstruction and state explicitly that the adapted weights are discarded and reset after every test input. They also show that the self-supervised signal is not always helpful, since rotation prediction lowers accuracy on classes whose appearance does not change under rotation. \citet{wang2021tent} define fully test-time adaptation, in which only the trained model and unlabelled test data are available, and adapt only the normalisation statistics and affine parameters by minimising the entropy of predictions. Their ablation finds that adapting all parameters never improves on the unadapted model, and they report a shift, from MNIST to SVHN, on which their method raises the error. \citet{karmanov2024efficient} show that adaptation need not use gradients at all. Their method for vision-language models keeps caches of test features with pseudo-labels, and its accuracy falls when the negative cache is made larger, because the extra entries accumulate noisy pseudo-labels.

Continual test-time adaptation drops the reset entirely. In the formulation surveyed by \citet{maharana2026continual}, a source model is updated online over a sequence of unlabelled target distributions with unknown boundaries and is never reset. The survey names two failure modes, catastrophic forgetting of the source model's knowledge and error accumulation from noisy self-generated targets, and describes stochastic parameter restoration, which returns a random subset of adapted weights to their source values at every step to bound long-horizon drift. The vision literature therefore already contains the question of this thesis in another form, and its answer is that persistence is possible but has to be bounded.

\subsection{Test-time training in language models}

\citet{sun2025learning} carry test-time training into sequence modelling. Their TTT layers make the hidden state itself a model, updated by a gradient step on a self-supervised reconstruction loss for each token or mini-batch. Two instantiations, TTT-Linear and TTT-MLP, wrap the inner model in layer normalisation and a residual connection. Mini-batch updates give the largest single improvement in their ablation, and a learnable, input-dependent step size acts as a gate on the size of each update. They prove that TTT-Linear with batch gradient descent and a zero initial state is equivalent to linear attention, and state that DeltaNet corresponds to TTT-Linear with a mini-batch of one, without the normalisation and residual. As we read the method, each sequence starts again from the learned initial state, so nothing is carried between sequences.

\citet{behrouz2025titans} use test-time learning as a long-term memory. Titans trains a neural memory module online with an associative loss, using an update that combines momentum on a surprise signal with an adaptive weight decay that acts as a forget gate, and in their ablation weight decay and momentum are the two components that contribute most. In its memory-as-context variant, retrieved memory is concatenated with the current segment before attention. All of its experiments run within a single continuous context per example, up to more than two million tokens, and none carries the memory across separately bounded documents.

\citet{tandon2025endtoend} meta-learn the initial state of the fast weights end to end, so that the initialisation is optimised for the loss after test-time updates rather than before them. Their fast weights are the MLP layers of the last quarter of the blocks, updated by next-token gradient steps every thousand tokens, and a second static MLP per updated block is kept as safe storage against forgetting. The paper discards training documents shorter than 8K tokens specifically to avoid resetting the updated MLPs at document boundaries when documents are packed together, so persistence across documents is neither attempted nor evaluated. On needle-in-a-haystack retrieval full attention dramatically outperforms every test-time training and recurrent method they test, which suggests that test-time training compresses context rather than storing it losslessly.

\citet{bansal2025letsnot} argue that static softmax attention suffers from score dilution, where the attention mass on a target falls as near-tie distractors accumulate with context length. Their query-only TTT keeps the key-value cache of one forward pass frozen and takes a few gradient steps on the query projections only, using next-token loss on short spans of the same prompt. It improves long-context benchmarks over in-context learning and compute-matched thinking, but the update belongs to one prompt and is not carried to the next. \citet{akyurek2025surprising} apply test-time training to few-shot reasoning with LoRA adapters trained per task on augmented leave-one-out versions of the demonstrations and discarded afterwards. One of their comparisons bears on this thesis. A separate adapter per task beats one shared adapter on ARC, where tasks interfere, while a shared adapter helps on BIG-Bench Hard.

\subsection{In-Place TTT and its successors}

In-Place TTT \citep{feng2026inplace} is the direct starting point of this thesis. It repurposes the final projection of a gated MLP, the down-projection, as the fast weight and leaves the gate and up projections frozen, so it can be added to a pretrained model without a new layer. For each chunk it applies the current fast weight and then updates it, and with its similarity loss the update reduces to an outer product of a target with the MLP activation, a Hebbian accumulation with no error term. The target is not a reconstruction of the input, as in earlier test-time training, but a causal depthwise convolution of the input followed by a learned projection, aligned with next-token prediction, and the authors prove that under their assumptions a reconstruction target has a negligible expected effect on the logit of the correct next token. For long sequences with a continually pretrained Qwen3-4B model they clip each chunk's update by its Frobenius norm to keep the fast weights stable. In-Place TTT improves the RULER accuracy of Qwen3-4B at long context, with a gain that widens with length and a slight loss at 4K. At document boundaries the fast weights are reset to their pretrained state to prevent leakage between independent sequences, and no partial carry or decay is tested. The paper does not compare against LoRA.

\citet{wang2026learning} replace the In-Place target with test-time context distillation. A long-window teacher and a short-window student share the frozen backbone, and the fast weight in the same down-projection is updated to move the student's MLP output towards the teacher's, so the write is driven by the residual between the two, an error-correcting update rather than a Hebbian one. They prove that under their assumptions an update made on an earlier in-context episode moves the student's state at a later related query towards the teacher's solution, and they report large gains over In-Place TTT on long-context retrieval. All of their evaluation, too, stays within the chunks of one long sequence.

\citet{ouyang2026ttt} keep the same fast weight in the down-projection and the same chunk-parallel rank-one writes, but replace the convolutional target with the layer's own hidden state at the next position. Like our Hebbian rule, their training-time write drops the error term so that it can be summed in parallel across chunks. Under matched continual pretraining they find that the original In-Place TTT target does not improve RULER over the released backbone, while their target does, for four models including Qwen3-0.6B. They restore the original weight after every sample, so this line of work, too, never carries the fast weight to a new input.

\subsection{What test-time training is not}

Two neighbouring ideas share the words test time. In-context learning, demonstrated at scale with GPT-3 \citep{brown2020language}, adapts a frozen model through its context alone, with no gradient update, and is bounded by the context window \citep{dong2024survey}. A line of theory shows that in-context learning can carry out a learning algorithm inside the forward pass. \citet{vonoswald2023transformers} construct a linear self-attention layer whose forward pass is exactly one step of gradient descent on a regression loss built from the context, and find that trained transformers converge to this solution, while \citet{akyurek2023learning} show that transformers can implement gradient descent and closed-form ridge regression and that the algorithm they match changes with depth and width. In both cases the adaptation lives in activations and vanishes with the context. In the evaluation of this thesis each slice is read without the previous text in attention, so the carried state is the only channel between slices and a gain from carrying it cannot come from in-context learning over earlier text. Test-time scaling, in turn, spends more decoding compute with frozen weights. \citet{muennighoff2025s1} control the length of reasoning with budget forcing and find that more thinking helps up to a point and then flattens. Neither idea changes the model's parameters, which is what defines test-time training.

\section{Fast weights and associative memory}
\label{sec-lit-fw}

The idea of weights that change quickly at inference time is older than transformers. \citet{french1999catastrophic} reviews the dual-weight scheme of Hinton and Plaut, in which a fast weight changes quickly and decays quickly towards zero while a slow weight changes and decays slowly, with the effective weight combining the two, and notes that the scheme had not been thoroughly explored and may be limited in capacity. \citet{ba2016using} revive fast weights in recurrent networks with the rule $A(t) = \lambda A(t-1) + \eta\, h(t) h(t)^\top$, a decayed outer product of the hidden state with itself. They choose this one-shot Hebbian storage over iterative error-correcting storage for simplicity, note that outer-product storage holds far fewer patterns than error-correcting rules can, and need layer normalisation inside the inner loop to keep the dynamics stable. They describe the result as a kind of attention to the recent past.

The connection to transformers is exact. \citet{katharopoulos2020transformers} replace the softmax with a kernel feature map and rewrite causal linear attention as a recurrent network whose state is updated by adding the outer product of each key and value feature, with a normaliser accumulated in the same way, which brings the cost from quadratic to linear in sequence length. \citet{schlag2021linear} show that this state is a fast-weight matrix in the sense of Schmidhuber's fast weight programmers, written by $W^{(i)} = W^{(i-1)} + v^{(i)} \otimes k^{(i)}$, and \citet{yang2024gla} make the same identification for matrix-valued recurrent states in general. The fast weight of In-Place TTT, written by an outer product of a target and an activation, belongs to this family.

Reading the state as an associative memory explains its limits. \citet{ramsauer2021hopfield} show that softmax attention is one update step of a modern continuous Hopfield network, whose storage capacity is exponential in the pattern dimension, and that when stored patterns are not well separated, retrieval settles on an average of similar patterns rather than on one of them. \citet{schlag2021linear} show that an additive matrix memory can hold at most as many associations as its key dimension without retrieval error. \citet{mcdermott2026lifelong} frame every attention mechanism as test-time regression, an online estimator from keys to values. In their taxonomy softmax attention is nonparametric, with the growing key-value cache as the estimator, while linear attention, state-space models, fast-weight programmers and TTT layers are parametric, with a fixed-size state updated online. They classify update rules into a Hebbian rule, a delta rule and an Omega rule that fits a window of past pairs, note that under the Hebbian rule the number of storable associations is bounded by the rank of the state, and argue that instantaneous updates give no guarantee that earlier associations survive.

Explicit memories offer a contrast. The differentiable neural computer \citep{graves2016hybrid} couples a controller to an external memory matrix that it reads by content and writes with an explicit erase-then-write operation, with a usage vector to allocate free slots and a temporal link matrix to recall in the order of writing. Its memory is separate from the network's weights and has a designed forgetting mechanism, unlike a fast weight updated by pure accumulation. \citet{irie2022modern} go the other way and let one self-referential weight matrix generate its own updates with the delta rule, collapsing the hierarchy between slow and fast weights.

\section{Update rules, gating and stability}
\label{sec-lit-rules}

\subsection{Hebbian and delta rules}

The update rule decides both what a fast weight can store and how it behaves when it keeps being written. The additive, or Hebbian, rule adds every new outer product in full, whether it agrees with what is stored or not. \citet{schlag2021linear} show that such a rule cannot overwrite a stale association, since binding a key to a new value only adds interference, and propose the delta rule, which retrieves the value currently stored for a key and writes the difference between the new value and the retrieved one, with a learned write strength. On WikiText-103 the delta rule lowers test perplexity by 1.5 to 2.8 points at matched size. \citet{yang2024deltanet} call the failure of the additive rule key collision, which appears once the sequence is longer than the state dimension because nothing removes old associations. They derive the delta rule as one step of online gradient descent on a regression loss, so that the update is proportional to the prediction error, whereas the implicit loss of linear attention has a gradient that does not depend on the error at all. With normalised keys the transition matrix of the delta rule has eigenvalues between zero and one, so the state cannot grow without bound. \citet{tiezzi2025state} survey the wider family, from linear attention to DeltaNet and its gated successors, describe the delta rule explicitly as error-correcting, and add a second limitation, that recurrences whose transition matrices have only positive eigenvalues cannot solve certain state-tracking tasks.

Error correction is not a free win. \citet{mao2022finetuning} fine-tunes a pretrained GPT-2 into recurrent fast-weight models with four update rules. Under its best configuration the plain additive rule slightly outperforms the delta rule, which the author attributes to unstable recurrent dynamics during fine-tuning, and the delta rule diverges unless its features are sum-normalised and its gate starts very small. Gated and decaying rules beat both, and the additive rule is described as unable to forget, so that it can overwhelm a fixed state. \citet{yang2025gated} report that DeltaNet alone performs only moderately on real-world tasks and attribute this to the lack of a mechanism that clears outdated information. They combine a global decay gate with the delta rule and show that the two are complementary. The gate erases memory quickly and uniformly, the delta term modifies it precisely, and in their needle-in-a-haystack study decay alone loses stored information while the delta rule alone suffers memory collisions on long real text.

\subsection{Gating and selectivity}

A gate or a decay lets a state forget. \citet{yang2024gla} quantify how much this matters in a controlled ablation. Linear attention with no gate reaches a training perplexity of 23.21, against 16.55 with a fixed scalar decay, 15.56 with a data-dependent scalar gate and 14.77 with their fine-grained gated linear attention, and they cite the hypothesis that the lack of a decay contributes to the instability of linear attention on long contexts. \citet{gu2024mamba} make the parameters of a state-space model depend on the input and show that in the simplest case this reduces to a classic recurrent gate that decides whether to keep the state or overwrite it with the current input. In their ablation the selective step size is the largest single source of improvement. They also discuss resetting at boundaries directly, observing that when documents are packed together a time-invariant model bleeds information from one sequence into the next, whereas a selective model can reset its state. \citet{beck2024xlstm} write the matrix memory of the mLSTM as $C_t = f_t C_{t-1} + i_t v_t k_t^\top$, a fast-weight update with learned, input-dependent forget and input gates and a normaliser state, and show with a toy task that a sigmoid-gated memory fails to revise a stored value when a more relevant key arrives later, while exponential gating fixes this. RWKV \citep{peng2023rwkv} keeps a running sum of values with a learned per-channel exponential decay, and its authors note that funnelling all past context through one fixed-size state limits the recall of fine detail over long contexts.

Learned decay need not act on tokens only. \citet{ravi2017optimization} cast gradient descent as the cell update of an LSTM whose forget gate becomes a learned, per-parameter decay of the accumulated weights and whose input gate becomes a learned step size, with plain gradient descent as the special case of a constant step and no decay. The adaptive weight decay of Titans \citep{behrouz2025titans} plays the same role inside a test-time memory. Structure matters as much as the presence of a gate. \citet{gu2022efficiently} show that a state-space model initialised with the HiPPO matrix beats one initialised randomly by more than 15 points on sequential CIFAR-10, and that an earlier model using the same matrix without their reparameterisation was numerically unstable.

\subsection{Stabilising a growing state}

Every family above needs some mechanism to keep its state in a usable range. Linear attention divides its read by an accumulated normaliser \citep{katharopoulos2020transformers}, and \citet{schlag2021linear} replace that normaliser with a sum normalisation because a normaliser that always grows can make training unstable. Fast weights in recurrent networks use layer normalisation \citep{ba2016using}, TTT layers wrap their inner model in normalisation and a residual \citep{sun2025learning}, the mLSTM divides its read by a normaliser state \citep{beck2024xlstm}, and In-Place TTT clips the Frobenius norm of each update \citep{feng2026inplace}. Large-chunk TTT states the problem directly, that accumulated fast-weight updates suffer from magnitude explosion or decayed memory, and normalises the fast weights after every update \citep{zhang2025test}. Generative Adapter, which builds a weight update from outer products of hidden states, found such weights unstable and resets all of their singular values to one \citep{chen2024generative}. Dynamic evaluation pulls the adapted parameters back towards the trained ones at every step \citep{krause2018dynamic}. These mechanisms differ in what they discard. A decay or a pull-back keeps the relative scale of recent writes, while a norm bound applied to the state once it is large keeps only its direction, which is the property Chapter~\ref{ch-why} examines. The normalisations of large-chunk TTT and Generative Adapter also keep only direction, and both methods work, so discarding magnitude does not by itself make a fast weight useless.

\section{Memory and persistence across segments and documents}
\label{sec-lit-persist}

Several architectures keep information beyond the current segment, and they differ in where they keep it and in whether they cross document boundaries. Transformer-XL \citep{dai2019transformerxl} caches the hidden states of the previous segment with a stop-gradient and lets the next segment attend to them, so what persists is a cache of activations computed with fixed weights, and no parameter is updated. An ablation on a sentence-shuffled corpus shows that the recurrence helps even without long-range dependency, simply by removing the fragmentation of context at segment edges. The Compressive Transformer \citep{rae2019compressive} compresses the oldest cached activations into a second memory instead of discarding them. It is trained without resetting its memories, so they persist across article boundaries, and its authors observe that the model is slightly out of sample when given short contexts with empty memories. Discussing their optimisation schedule, they note that updating parameters only at article boundaries and resetting the state could help long-range memory models but would slow learning, and they report that even a naive dynamic evaluation over the test set improves their WikiText-103 perplexity from 17.1 to 16.1.

The Recurrent Memory Transformer \citep{bulatov2022rmt} passes learned memory tokens from one segment to the next and trains them by backpropagation through several segments, and deeper unrolling is what makes the memory useful. Memorizing Transformers \citep{wu2022memorizing} retrieve the keys and values of past tokens from a large non-differentiable memory by nearest-neighbour search and combine the result with local attention through a learned gate. The memory is cleared at the start of each new document, it helps most on rare tokens such as names, and the authors note that stored keys become stale as the parameters that produced them drift during training. StreamingLLM \citep{xiao2024efficient} keeps a few initial attention-sink tokens and a rolling window of recent keys and values, which gives stable perplexity over streams of millions of tokens with no fine-tuning. Its authors evaluate on a concatenation of books and observe perplexity fluctuations at book transitions, and their ablation shows that a larger cache does not consistently lower perplexity.

Several recent methods compress earlier text into a compact memory that a later input can use. AutoCompressors \citep{chevalier2023adapting} turn each segment into a few summary vectors that later segments read as soft prompts, trained by next-token loss with gradients stopped after two compression steps. Although they are trained only within documents, the summary vectors of separately retrieved documents can be combined and still help. Infini-attention \citep{munkhdalai2024leave} keeps a compressive associative memory in each attention head across the segments of one long input, written with a Hebbian or a delta rule and mixed with local attention through a learned gate. MemoryLLM \citep{wang2024memoryllm} keeps a fixed pool of memory tokens in every layer, updated by forward passes over a stream of texts with a designed exponential forgetting, and Larimar \citep{das2024larimar} adds an external episodic memory that is written in one shot by a least-squares solution, for fast editing of facts. Generative Adapter \citep{chen2024generative} maps a stream of context chunks to a low-rank weight update in a single forward pass by accumulating outer products of hidden states, while Cartridges \citep{eyuboglu2025cartridges} instead train a compact key-value cache offline for each corpus and match in-context quality with 38.6 times less memory. Where these methods are compared with keeping the full text in context, the text often remains more accurate. On multi-session conversations, for example, prompting with the full history reaches an F1 of 66.0 against 40.2 for Generative Adapter \citep{chen2024generative}. None of these methods places the carried state in a feed-forward fast weight, and none asks how its effect on a later document depends on how the two documents relate.

Persistence of fast weights is rarer. \citet{schlag2021linear} carry the fast-weight state across the training segments of WikiText-103 without truncation. With the additive rule this breaks, reaching a perplexity above 1600, while the delta rule carries stably and is competitive with Transformer-XL with a much smaller state. \citet{irie2022modern} report the opposite ablation, in which resetting the self-modifying weights after every fixed span, instead of carrying them across an episode, removes the benefit of self-modification. In test-time training the reset is the rule. Per-sample resets are a stated design choice in vision \citep{sun2020ttt,gandelsman2022ttt}, TTT layers reset per sequence \citep{sun2025learning}, In-Place TTT and end-to-end TTT reset at document boundaries \citep{feng2026inplace,tandon2025endtoend}, and Titans and context distillation stay within one context \citep{behrouz2025titans,wang2026learning}. \citet{tiezzi2025state} note that learning online from an unsegmented, potentially unbounded stream without resets remains largely unexplored for recurrent models. \citet{kim2026delayed} argue that next-token training gives a test-time memory little reason to keep information through many later updates, and add a delayed question-answering loss whose gradient reaches the earlier writes, which improves long-context retrieval within one stream.

\section{Efficient and long-context transformers}
\label{sec-lit-long}

Most work on long context extends what a single forward pass can see. Self-attention costs time quadratic in sequence length \citep{vaswani2017attention}, which motivates a large family of efficient transformers surveyed by \citet{fournier2023practical}. Sparse attention, such as the combination of random, windowed and global tokens in BigBird, reduces the cost to linear while keeping the model a universal approximator \citep{zaheer2020bigbird}, although its authors prove that some tasks need far more layers with sparse attention than with full attention. Reformer approximates attention with locality-sensitive hashing and saves memory with reversible layers \citep{kitaev2020reformer}. Kernel methods make attention linear and recurrent \citep{katharopoulos2020transformers}. FlashAttention keeps attention exact but avoids materialising the full score matrix, which reduces memory to linear in sequence length and makes longer training contexts affordable \citep{dao2022flashattention}. Grouped-query attention shares key and value heads across groups of query heads to shrink the cache during decoding \citep{ainslie2023gqa}, and H2O evicts all but a small set of heavily attended tokens and the most recent ones \citep{zhang2023h2o}. YaRN \citep{peng2023yarn} and LongRoPE \citep{ding2024longrope} extend the context window by rescaling rotary position embeddings, with no persistent weight update. Long Range Arena \citep{tay2020long} compares many of these models on tasks of 1K to 16K tokens and finds that none dominates and that all of them fail its longest task.

Recurrence offers a different route. The Universal Transformer applies the same block repeatedly over depth with adaptive halting \citep{dehghani2019universal}, whereas linear attention and fast weights recur over sequence positions \citep{katharopoulos2020transformers}. State-space models such as S4 \citep{gu2022efficiently} and Mamba \citep{gu2024mamba} replace attention with a fixed-size recurrent state, and the survey of \citet{tiezzi2025state} shows that such models cannot copy long inputs unless their state grows with the sequence, and that selection closes much of their gap to attention on recall probes. All of these methods make one long input cheaper or longer. None keeps anything after the input ends, which is the setting of this thesis, so they are complementary to a carried fast weight rather than alternatives to it.

\section{Continual learning, forgetting and meta-learning}
\label{sec-lit-cl}

A state carried from one document to the next is a form of continual learning and inherits its central problem. \citet{french1999catastrophic} describes catastrophic forgetting, in which learning new patterns abruptly erases old ones in a network with shared distributed weights, as the price of the overlapping representations that let such networks generalise, the stability and plasticity dilemma. The classic remedies fall into three groups. Replay rehearses old data or, in deep generative replay, samples from a generator trained on it \citep{shin2017continual}, and in that study replaying random noise instead of reconstructed inputs fails to prevent forgetting. Regularisation anchors the parameters, as elastic weight consolidation does with a quadratic penalty weighted by the Fisher information of each parameter \citep{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}. Combined methods such as iCaRL use exemplar rehearsal with distillation, and show that plain fine-tuning on a stream of classes collapses to near zero accuracy on the early classes \citep{rebuffi2017icarl}. In large language models, \citet{li2024revisiting} link forgetting during sequential fine-tuning to the sharpness of the loss landscape, reduce it with sharpness-aware minimisation, and find that forgetting grows with model size. They confine their study to fine-tuning and leave continual updates after deployment open. \citet{oneill2026continually} write invented facts one at a time into a model already changed by earlier writes. After twenty writes the model answers between 1 and 46 percent of questions about earlier facts, depending mostly on how the later facts were written, and an earlier fact's raised log-probability can remain after the model no longer answers questions about it.

Surveys of continual learning for language models organise the field by training stage. \citet{shi2024continual} distinguish continual pretraining, domain-adaptive pretraining and continual fine-tuning, list standard measures of forgetting and of backward and forward transfer, and report that language models resist forgetting at the level of representations more than at the level of outputs. \citet{zheng2024towards} cover twelve lifelong-learning scenarios, all defined by training-time updates or by external retrieval and tools, and do not treat test-time parameter adaptation at all. Session-persistent test-time training falls outside both taxonomies.

Meta-learning offers another lens. \citet{hospedales2020meta} formalise it as a bilevel problem in which an outer loop learns knowledge that an inner loop uses to solve each task, and flag generalisation across narrow or diverse task families as an open challenge. MAML \citep{finn2017model} meta-learns an initialisation from which a few gradient steps adapt to a new task, an idea that end-to-end TTT applies to fast weights \citep{tandon2025endtoend}, and the meta-learned optimiser of \citet{ravi2017optimization} learns the update rule itself. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning is the practical counterpart. LoRA \citep{hu2022lora} freezes a weight matrix and learns a low-rank additive update whose second factor is zero-initialised, so the adapted model starts identical to the base model, and in the original study it is applied to attention projections while the MLP is left frozen. \citet{hou2022meta} make the low-rank factors functions of the input and meta-learn them, and find that meta-learning pays off only when it starts from a pretrained model.

\section{Dynamic evaluation and adaptation of language models}
\label{sec-lit-dyneval}

Inference-time adaptation in language modelling predates neural networks. \citet{kneser1993dynamic} interpolate fifteen category-specific bigram models and one general model and re-estimate the mixture weights online by expectation maximisation over a window of recent words, a gradient-free scheme that lowers perplexity by ten percent. In their example the weight of the current category appears to rise during a stretch of text and to fall back after a change, which suggests that the state tracks the present category rather than building up across categories. Dynamic evaluation \citep{krause2018dynamic} adapts the parameters of a neural model by gradient descent on each segment of the test sequence as it is read, distinguishing the local distribution of the current sequence from the global distribution learned in training. Its best update rescales gradients with statistics from training and adds a decay that pulls the parameters back towards their trained values. A sparse variant adapts only a small matrix acting on a subset of hidden units and recovers most of the full method's gain with a fraction of its parameters. The benefit of adaptation rises and then levels off within a few thousand characters of in-distribution text, while on out-of-distribution text it keeps growing. \citet{irie2022modern} relate fast-weight self-modification to dynamic evaluation, noting that a self-referential model could also adapt the adaptation algorithm itself.

\citet{lazaridou2021mind} apply dynamic evaluation to temporal drift. A model trained only on older data underperforms one trained on data overlapping the test period, by a margin that grows with the time gap and that a larger model does not close. Dynamic evaluation narrows this gap, with its largest gains on newly emerging words, but it forgets older data as it adapts to newer data, and the authors conclude that it does not fully solve temporal generalisation. Their analysis of emerging words also separates two kinds of memory. A second occurrence of a new word becomes much easier only if the first is still within the attention cache, and outside it the model gains little, which suggests that information which has left the context has to be kept by some other means. Dynamic evaluation therefore already offers a way to carry adaptation from earlier text, but by gradient steps on all or part of the slow weights rather than by a fast weight built in the forward pass, and Chapter~\ref{ch-design} compares the two directly.

\section{Evaluation methodology}
\label{sec-lit-eval}

Perplexity on held-out text is the standard measure in this literature \citep{krause2018dynamic,lazaridou2021mind}, but it has known limits. \citet{meister2021beyond} argue for also testing whether generated text reproduces the statistical tendencies of natural language, recommend permutation tests that assume no distribution, and warn that with very large samples almost every test becomes significant even for trivial effects, so effect sizes must be reported. Long-context benchmarks probe the use of context more directly. RULER \citep{hsieh2024ruler} extends needle-in-a-haystack retrieval with multi-hop tracing, aggregation and question answering at controllable lengths, finds that most models fall well short of their claimed context length and that RWKV-v5-7B and Mamba-2.8B trail a Llama2-7B Transformer and degrade sharply by 8K, and describes itself as a convenient behavioural check rather than a target. LongBench \citep{bai2023longbench} adds a length-balanced subset that separates long-context ability from task skill, and a diagnostic that withholds the context to measure how much of a score comes from memorisation rather than from use of the context.

\citet{song2026beyond} argue that perplexity answers a narrower question than claims of memory. They separate stream or domain adaptation, measured by future-token loss, from bridge evidence that information is stored in model state and from behaviour after the original context is removed, and they call the use of lower-level evidence for a higher-level claim evidence migration. They place In-Place TTT and related layers at the first level. In a controlled test, one-step updates lowered the loss on injected facts at three model scales while free-form recall stayed at zero. They recommend comparing a carried stream with a reset at each item, and matching memory claims against in-context and retrieval baselines.

Three methodological lessons from this literature shape the protocol of this thesis. First, a gain must be attributed to the mechanism that produces it, as the fragmentation ablation of Transformer-XL \citep{dai2019transformerxl} and the results on in-context learning above \citep{vonoswald2023transformers,akyurek2023learning} show, which is why the evaluation separates training gain, within-document gain and carried gain in paired regimes. Second, effects in this area are typically small, as the modest gain of the first test-time training paper on a real shift \citep{sun2020ttt} and the modest margins reported for self-modifying fast weights \citep{irie2022modern} show, so intervals and win rates matter more than significance alone \citep{meister2021beyond}. Third, persistence must be tested at the boundaries where it is supposed to act, which earlier methods either avoid by clearing their memory per document \citep{wu2022memorizing} or observe only in passing, as in the book transitions of \citet{xiao2024efficient}. The continual-learning measures of backward and forward transfer \citep{shi2024continual,zheng2024towards} formalise the same idea of measuring what earlier material does to later performance.

Three patterns emerge from the literature. First, every test-time training method for language models resets its adapted state at document boundaries or never leaves a single context. Where persistence across inputs has been studied, in online and continual test-time adaptation in vision \citep{sun2020ttt,maharana2026continual}, in recurrent memories that cross the segments of one stream \citep{dai2019transformerxl,rae2019compressive,bulatov2022rmt,schlag2021linear}, and in dynamic evaluation \citep{krause2018dynamic,lazaridou2021mind}, it either assumes a homogeneous stream, carries activations rather than a fast weight, or carries slow weights updated by gradient steps. Memories built by forward passes, such as MemoryLLM and Generative Adapter, do persist along a stream of texts \citep{wang2024memoryllm,chen2024generative}, but they are separate modules or generated adapters, and they are evaluated on recall of that stream rather than on transfer to unrelated documents.

Second, the update-rule literature predicts that a carried fast weight will be hard to keep useful. A purely additive state accumulates interference and cannot overwrite \citep{schlag2021linear,yang2024deltanet}, an ungated one cannot forget \citep{yang2024gla,mao2022finetuning}, a state without selectivity cannot filter irrelevant history \citep{gu2024mamba}, and an adapted model that is never reset accumulates errors and forgets \citep{maharana2026continual,lazaridou2021mind}. The designs that work add decay, gating, error correction or a training signal that crosses the boundary \citep{yang2025gated,beck2024xlstm,bulatov2022rmt,chevalier2023adapting,kim2026delayed}.

Third, whether adaptation on one input helps another depends on how the two relate. In the convex case, test-time training provably helps when its gradient is aligned with that of the main task \citep{sun2020ttt}, and can hurt when its signal is misaligned \citep{gandelsman2022ttt}, and whether one adapter shared across tasks helps or interferes depends on the tasks \citep{akyurek2025surprising}.

The gap is therefore specific. No prior work carries a feed-forward fast weight across independently bounded documents, measures what such a carry contributes against a reset at the boundary, or asks how its effect depends on the relatedness of the earlier text. This thesis adds such a carry to an In-Place TTT design, compares a Hebbian and a delta update rule because the literature predicts they behave differently under persistence, compares the carry with dynamic evaluation as the established way of carrying adaptation between texts, and measures within-document and cross-document effects separately with paired regimes and bootstrap intervals.

\section{In-Place TTT on the MLP down-projection}

Every transformer block applies a position-wise feed-forward MLP after attention \citep{vaswani2017attention}, and In-Place TTT \citep{feng2026inplace} uses the final, down-projection matrix of this MLP as its fast weight. Let $z \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{ff}}$ be the gated activation of a transformer MLP and $W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d_{ff}}$ its down-projection. In-Place TTT adds a fast weight $S$ of the same shape, built during the forward pass, and a learned scalar gate $g(h) = \sigma(w_g^\top h + b_g)$ computed from the layer input $h$,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{out} = W_0 z + g(h)\, \eta\, S z .
  \label{eq-out}
\end{equation}
The fast weight is written in chunks of $C$ tokens. For chunk $k$ the layer forms a target $V_k$ from a causal depthwise convolution of the normalised hidden states followed by a learned projection $W_{\mathrm{target}}$. $W_{\mathrm{target}}$ is zero-initialised, so every TTT layer is identical to the base MLP at the start of training. Chunk $k$ reads only the state accumulated from strictly earlier chunks, which keeps the layer causal. Figure~\ref{fig-arch} shows the layer.

\section{Update rules}

\paragraph{Hebbian.} The Hebbian rule, the form used by In-Place TTT and by linear attention \citep{feng2026inplace,katharopoulos2020transformers}, writes an outer product with no error term,
\begin{equation}
  \Delta S_k = \tfrac{1}{C} V_k^\top Z_k, \qquad S_k = S_{\mathrm{carried}} + \textstyle\sum_{j<k} \Delta S_j .
\end{equation}
Nothing in this write checks whether $S$ already predicts $V$, so the state grows with every chunk.

\paragraph{Chunked delta.} The chunked delta rule writes the residual of the current prediction, with the state frozen at the start of each chunk,
\begin{equation}
  \hat V_k = \frac{\eta}{1 + \lVert z \rVert^2}\, Z_k S_k^\top, \qquad \Delta S_k = \tfrac{1}{C} (V_k - \hat V_k)^\top Z_k ,
\end{equation}
with the normalised step $\eta / (1 + \lVert z \rVert^2)$ applied per token row. For this rule the read in Equation~\ref{eq-out} uses the same normalised step, so the TTT term is $\hat V_k$. The delta rule was introduced for fast weights by \citet{schlag2021linear} and amounts to a step of online gradient descent on a regression loss \citep{yang2024deltanet}. This is the mini-batch form of the delta rule used by \citet{sun2025learning}, and its error term lets a state that already predicts the target stop growing.

\section{Session persistence}

A session is a sequence of work items, either whole documents or slices of one document. At the end of each item the layer folds the item's total update into a carried state with an exponential moving average,
\begin{equation}
  S_{\mathrm{carried}} \leftarrow \lambda\, S_{\mathrm{carried}} + \Delta S_{\mathrm{item}}, \qquad \lambda = 0.9 ,
\end{equation}
and the next item starts from $S_{\mathrm{carried}}$ instead of from zero. This is the decayed accumulation that \citet{ba2016using} use for fast weights within a sequence, applied here between items. The carried state is detached between items, which limits backpropagation through time to one item. During training every session is a single document, so the carry is learned only between the slices of one document. Carrying it across documents is applied at evaluation, as a test of whether a state learned within documents transfers to the next one. Figure~\ref{fig-persist} contrasts this with resetting.

\section{Stabilisation}

Two mechanisms keep the fast weights stable. A Frobenius norm clip rescales the state whenever it is read, so that $\lVert \eta S \rVert_F \le \tau$ with $\tau = 20$, while preserving its direction. In-Place TTT similarly bounds the Frobenius norm of each chunk's update \citep{feng2026inplace}, while here the bound is applied to the state when it is read. For the Hebbian rule the clip acts only on the read, while for the delta rule the clipped state is also what the next chunk accumulates onto. The output gate is initialised with bias $b_g = -2$, so the TTT term enters at about 12 percent strength at the start of training and the model can learn to open or close it. Separately, the gradients of all trainable parameters are clipped to a total norm of 10. The read-time clip bounds the forward pass, including at evaluation where no gradients exist, while the gradient clip bounds each optimiser step. Chapter~\ref{ch-why} returns to the role of the read-time clip.

\section{Training objective and parameters}

All models are trained with the standard next-token cross-entropy. LoRA adapters \citep{hu2022lora} are trained in every model, so that the TTT models and the control differ only in the TTT layers. The TTT models additionally train the down-projection $W_0$ of the TTT layers and the new modules (target projection, convolution and gate). Counted from the layer shapes of Qwen3-0.6B, this gives about 68.0 million trainable parameters for a TTT model (9.2 million LoRA, 44.0 million in the 14 down-projections, 14.8 million in the new modules) against about 10.1 million for the LoRA-only control. This difference matters for interpreting the results and is discussed in Chapters~\ref{ch-q1} and~\ref{ch-limits}.

\section{Implementation}

The system is implemented in Python with PyTorch, Hugging Face Transformers and PEFT, and runs on cloud GPUs through Modal. It is organised in rings with inward-only dependencies, so that the training loop, the carry lifecycle and the evaluation suites run against in-memory fakes and are covered by a fast test suite without a GPU. Every evaluation writes a self-describing bundle of raw per-document rows, tables, figures and a manifest recording the exact configuration and code version, and every table and figure in this thesis is regenerated from those bundles without a GPU (Appendix~\ref{app-repro}).

\chapter{Experimental Setup and Evaluation Protocol}
\label{ch-eval}

\section{Model and data}

All models start from the pretrained Qwen3-0.6B model. TTT layers replace the MLPs of the 14 odd-numbered layers, 1, 3 and so on up to 27. Training and evaluation use the SlimPajama-6B subset \citep{yoon2023slimpajama6b} of SlimPajama \citep{soboleva2023slimpajama}, a cleaned and deduplicated derivative of RedPajama-V1 \citep{weber2024redpajama}, restricted to six RedPajama sources, namely ArXiv, Book, C4, Github, StackExchange and Wikipedia. Training uses 4000 documents of at least 2048 tokens, truncated to 16384 tokens and balanced across sources towards a target mix of 25 percent C4, 20 percent each of Github and ArXiv, 15 percent Book, and 10 percent each of Wikipedia and StackExchange. Each session is one document, read whole if shorter than 2100 tokens and otherwise split into two to six slices of at least 1000 tokens.

\section{Training}

All runs use LoRA of rank 16 with $\alpha = 32$ and dropout 0.05, a LoRA learning rate of $10^{-5}$, AdamW, 16 gradient accumulation steps, a warmup of 2 percent of steps, cosine decay over the full epoch of 1050 optimiser steps, and seed 42. TTT runs train their down-projections at $3 \times 10^{-5}$ and their new modules at $6 \times 10^{-2}$. Checkpoints at steps 200, 400 and 600 are evaluated. Because the epoch length depends only on the data and the session strategy, all runs share the same 1050-step schedule and data order. Appendix~\ref{app-hparams} lists every setting.

\section{Models compared}

\begin{description}
  \item[LoRA-only.] The matched control, with no TTT layers.
  \item[Hebbian.] TTT layers with the Hebbian rule.
  \item[Delta.] TTT layers with the chunked delta rule.
\end{description}
In the figures, which are generated directly from the result bundles, these models carry the labels \texttt{hebbian} and \texttt{delta\_chunk}, and plain Hebbian denotes the original Hebbian model.

\section{Held-out data}

The held-out set is drawn from documents never used in training. It is drawn from the last 15{,}000 rows of the dataset, which are removed before any training stage, and long enough to cut into slices. It has 20 documents per source, except Book, which is rare in the subset and yields only 6, giving 106 documents and 656{,}331 tokens. Every model is evaluated on exactly the same documents.

\section{Evaluation regimes}

Each document is cut into 8 equal slices and measured in four regimes (Table~\ref{tab-regimes}).

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{Evaluation regimes, each measured on every held-out document.}
  \label{tab-regimes}
  \begin{tabular}{llll}
    \toprule
    Regime & LoRA & Fast-weight updates & Carried between slices \\
    \midrule
    base & off & off, pretrained $W_0$ & no \\
    TTT off & on & off & no \\
    TTT, reset per slice & on & on & no \\
    TTT, carried & on & on & yes \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Differences between regimes give the decomposition reported throughout.
\begin{description}
  \item[Training gain.] Base minus TTT off. Everything the slow weights learned, including LoRA and, for TTT models, the trained $W_0$.
  \item[Within-document gain.] TTT off minus TTT reset per slice. What the fast weights learn inside a slice.
  \item[Between-slice gain.] TTT reset per slice minus TTT carried. What carrying state from earlier slices of the same document adds.
  \item[Matched gain.] The LoRA-only model minus the TTT model with its fast weights carried. The total advantage over the control.
\end{description}

\section{Persistence suites}

Three further suites probe persistence directly.
\begin{description}
  \item[Boundary.] For each source, a chain of five documents is read under three arms, carrying state across documents, resetting at document boundaries, and resetting at every slice. The cross-document gain compares the first two arms on documents two to five of each chain (24 documents), and the cross-slice gain compares the last two on all 30 documents. The arm that resets at document boundaries follows the reset rule of In-Place TTT, so the cross-document gain compares our persistent method with a reset in the style of In-Place TTT on the same model, data and documents. It is not a reproduction of the original system, which uses chunks of 512 or 1024 tokens, clips each chunk's update rather than the state, has no output gate and no LoRA, and was evaluated on models of 4B parameters and more \citep{feng2026inplace}.
  \item[Repeat.] Each document is read ten times in a row with its carried state never reset. Each later reading is compared with the first, and the tenth reading is also compared with the second, which tests whether the carry keeps compounding after one exposure. This is the most favourable test possible for a carried state, since the state was built from the very document being read again.
  \item[Swap.] A target document is read after the state has been primed on two documents from the same source or from a different source, each read whole, and compared with reading it unprimed, to test whether any carried benefit is source-specific, as planned in the proposal.
\end{description}

\section{Statistics}

Perplexity within a document is token-weighted over its slices. A gap between two regimes is the difference of their geometric-mean perplexities over documents, positive when the second regime has lower perplexity. We report 95 percent percentile bootstrap intervals from 1000 resamples of documents, with both regimes resampled on the same indices, and the win rate, the fraction of documents on which the second regime is lower. A star marks an interval that excludes zero. Following \citet{meister2021beyond}, every result is reported as an effect size with its interval rather than as a significance test alone.

\section{Measurement integrity}

Two errors in the evaluation pipeline were found and fixed during the study, and both are reported for transparency. First, the in-training evaluation of the original runs ran with LoRA dropout active, which perturbed every regime slightly. The fix puts every evaluation, in training and final, in evaluation mode. All numbers in Chapters~\ref{ch-q1} to~\ref{ch-why} come from the final evaluation. Chapter~\ref{ch-design} also reports in-training evaluation points to describe training dynamics, and all of them come from runs made after the fix. Second, the base regime initially kept the trained $W_0$ of the TTT layers instead of the pretrained one, which understated the training gain of TTT models. The fixed pipeline restores the pretrained $W_0$, and earlier bundles were corrected by taking the base regime from the LoRA-only bundle, which contains the true base model on the same documents. Two sanity checks confirm the pipeline. The LoRA-only model, which has no fast weights, gives a within-document and between-slice gain of exactly 0.000 at every step, and the base-model perplexities are identical across all LoRA-only bundles.

\section{TTT models beat the matched control, mostly through capacity}

Both TTT models are better than the LoRA-only control at every checkpoint (Table~\ref{tab-main}, Figure~\ref{fig-gains}). The Hebbian model leads by 0.073 to 0.106 perplexity and the delta model by 0.191 to 0.232, and at step 600 both are better on all 106 documents.

Most of this lead is not test-time learning. A TTT model trains about 6.7 times as many parameters as the control, and its training gain, measured with the fast weights switched off, already exceeds that of the control (1.928 and 1.992 against 1.859 at step 600). At step 600 the Hebbian model's lead of 0.106 splits into 0.069 of extra training gain, 0.034 of within-document gain and 0.002 between slices, so about two thirds is capacity. The delta model's lead of 0.203 splits into 0.133, 0.058 and 0.012, the same proportion.

\section{Fast weights help within a document}

Switching the fast weights on inside a slice lowers perplexity at every checkpoint for both rules, on 72 to 100 percent of documents. The delta rule's within-document gain is 9 times the Hebbian gain at step 200, 4 times at step 400 and 1.7 times at step 600. It is largest early, 0.144 at step 200 and 0.276 at step 100, and settles near 0.06, which is consistent with the slow weights absorbing part of what the fast weights first provided.

The gain does not build up along a document (Figure~\ref{fig-position}). It is already present in the first slice, dips in the middle slices and shows no upward trend, so the fast weights adapt locally rather than accumulating knowledge of the document. Carrying state from earlier slices of the same document adds a small gain for the delta rule, 0.012 at step 600 and about 0.015 per slice from the second slice on. For the original designs, this is the only form of persistence found to help.

\section{A naive carry across documents contributes nothing measurable}

The cross-document gain lies between $-0.004$ and $+0.008$ perplexity at every checkpoint for both rules, and every interval includes zero (Table~\ref{tab-main}, Figure~\ref{fig-gains} right). Three times more training does not move it. Since the reset arm of the boundary suite follows the reset rule of In-Place TTT, this is also the comparison with a reset in that style. On the same model, data and documents, the naive carry adds nothing measurable to it. These intervals come from 24 documents and are about $\pm 0.01$ wide, so they rule out an effect larger than about 0.01 rather than proving that there is none, and Chapter~\ref{ch-design} measures the redesigned carry on about 100 documents.

Table~\ref{tab-persist} collects the persistence measures at step 600. Re-reading a document with its own carried state never helps. Against the first reading, the gain of every later reading up to the tenth is within 0.002 perplexity, and no interval excludes zero, for either rule (Figure~\ref{fig-repeat}). The tenth reading against the second is $+0.000$ with an interval of $\pm 0.001$ for both rules, even though the carried state was built from that very document. The swap suite (Figure~\ref{fig-swap}) shows that a foreign carried state can do harm. For the Hebbian model, a state primed on a different source raises perplexity in 15 of the 30 source pairs, by 0.022 on average and by up to 0.07, and never lowers it significantly, while same-source priming is close to neutral ($-0.008$). The Hebbian same-against-other effect of 0.010 in Table~\ref{tab-persist} therefore reflects a foreign state hurting less, not a same-source state helping. For the delta model, priming is neutral in either case, with 3 of 30 pairs significant in mixed directions. For the original designs, no carried state helps the next document.

\section{Answer to Q1}

For the naive carry, information retained from the previous document contributes nothing measurable to language model improvement in this setting, where the carry is learned within documents and applied across them. The improvement of a TTT model over a matched baseline comes from extra trainable capacity and from adaptation within each document, and the only persistence that helps is carrying state between slices of the same document with the delta rule. Chapter~\ref{ch-design} revisits this answer with a redesigned carry, which makes the carry between slices of the same document substantially useful and transfers to later documents of the same source, most to related ones, while it harms documents read after text of another source or after a long accumulated history.

For within-document adaptation, StackExchange and Wikipedia benefit most in relative terms and ArXiv, Book and Github least, and the delta rule helps every domain. \chapter{Why the Naive Carry Fails}
\label{ch-why}

\section{The read-time clip is always active}

The clip binds whenever $\lVert \eta S \rVert_F > \tau$. In units of the pretrained down-projection, this is a state ratio $\lVert \eta S \rVert_F / \lVert W_0 \rVert_F$ above $\tau / \lVert W_0 \rVert_F = 0.43$, given a mean $\lVert W_0 \rVert_F$ of 46.6 over the TTT layers. Across every boundary, repeat, swap and regime measurement at steps 200, 400 and 600, the state ratio is above this threshold on 100 percent of measurements, by a factor of at least 255 for the Hebbian model and at least 1597 for the delta model (Table~\ref{tab-clip}, Figure~\ref{fig-dist}).

\section{Only the direction of the state reaches the output}

A saturated clip means that the magnitude of the state never reaches the output. What reaches it is a vector of norm $\tau$ pointing along the accumulated state. Two observations follow. First, re-reading a document grows the carried state by a factor of about 6.5 over ten readings, from a median state ratio of about 14{,}600 after the first reading to about 95{,}000 after the tenth for the delta model, and from about 2{,}600 to 16{,}900 for the Hebbian model, while perplexity stays flat (Figure~\ref{fig-repeat}, which starts at the second reading). Second, carrying state into a new document makes it about two to four times larger than a state reset at the boundary, again with no effect on perplexity (Figure~\ref{fig-chain}). With $\lambda = 0.9$ the carried direction is an average over earlier documents, and once normalised to $\tau$ it holds nothing specific to the next one.

\section{Removing the clip does not help}

If saturation erases the information in the state's magnitude, removing the clip might let persistence work. We trained both rules without the clip, keeping every other setting, the data order and the gradient clip (Figure~\ref{fig-noclip}).

The Hebbian model collapses. It settles at a training loss of about 8.1, a perplexity of roughly 3300, and does not recover within the 200 steps run, while the clipped Hebbian model trains normally. Its early loss, 25.8 over the first 10 steps, is not by itself evidence, because a clipped Hebbian model can also start with a loss spike that it recovers from (Chapter~\ref{ch-design}). The unclipped delta model, reading the same data in the same order, stays between 1.8 and 2.8 throughout. Without an error term and with a fixed step, the Hebbian state grows with every chunk and wrecks the forward pass.

\section{The optimiser closes the gate whenever the fast weights are unusable}

The same response appears twice (Figure~\ref{fig-gates}). The clipped Hebbian model also has a nearly closed gate, with a median of at most 0.003 at every chain position, against about 0.78 for the clipped delta model, and the unclipped delta model closes its gate fully. Whenever the TTT term is too large or too noisy to use, training learns to shut it out.

\section{Summary}

The clip is what makes the fast weights usable at all, by reducing an unusably large state to a fixed and usable scale, and it does so by discarding the magnitude of the state. In the naive carry, whose write is never trained to serve a later read, the direction that remains is uninformative even about the document that produced it, and in two short single-seed probes removing the clip made the fast weights either unstable or unused. The cause of the naive null result is argued from the mechanism and supported by these probes, rather than shown by a single intervention. A saturated clip does not by itself rule out a useful carry. Chapter~\ref{ch-design} keeps the clip, which stays saturated, and instead changes how the carry is read and trained, so that the direction it carries is learned to be useful.

\chapter{A Carry That Works on Related Text}
\label{ch-design}

The previous chapters show that the carry, as first designed, contributes little even between slices of the same document, where the text it carries is directly relevant. Three features of the training setup are plausible causes. The carried state was detached between slices, so only the parts of the model that read the carry were trained, never the parts that decide what to write. The carry was added into the same sum as the current slice's state and clipped together with it, so the current slice could drown it. And the Hebbian rule starts training with a loss spike during which the fast weights are unusable. The saturated clip of Chapter~\ref{ch-why} is kept, so the redesign also tests whether a carry whose magnitude never reaches the output can still be useful once its write is trained. This chapter redesigns the carry against these three points, for the Hebbian rule, which is cheaper to train, and tests the result with the same evaluation protocol.

\section{Design}

All changes are configuration options, and every other setting is identical to the original Hebbian run.

\paragraph{A separate carry path.} The carried state is read through its own term with its own clip, gate and scale,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{out} = W_0 z + g(h)\, \eta\, S_{\mathrm{in}} z + \alpha\, g_c(h)\, \eta\, \mathrm{clip}(S_{\mathrm{carried}})\, z ,
\end{equation}
where $S_{\mathrm{in}}$ is the state built inside the current slice, $g_c$ is a second gate and $\alpha$ is a learnable scalar initialised to zero. As with the zero-initialised factor of LoRA \citep{hu2022lora}, the layer is therefore exactly the original Hebbian layer at initialisation, and the carried term enters only if training finds it useful.

\paragraph{Training through the carry.} The carried state is kept in the autograd graph between the slices of a session, so the loss on a later slice also trains what an earlier slice wrote. This follows the Recurrent Memory Transformer, whose memory becomes useful only when it is trained by backpropagation across segments \citep{bulatov2022rmt}. To bound memory, the carry is detached after every second slice and at every optimiser step. AutoCompressors make the same choice, stopping gradients after two compression steps with no loss of quality \citep{chevalier2023adapting}. Gradient checkpointing re-runs each layer's forward pass during the backward pass, and with the carry in the graph a layer can be re-run after its carry has moved on, which silently corrupts the gradient. The recompute is therefore given the carry the layer actually read, and a test confirms that gradients with and without checkpointing are identical, and that they differ without this correction.

\paragraph{A nearly closed main gate at the start.} The main gate's bias is initialised at $-6$ instead of $-2$, so the in-document fast weights start at about 0.25 percent strength rather than 12 percent. This is close to where the original Hebbian model's gate ends after training (0.002).

\section{Two failed attempts}

The design above is the third attempt, and the first two explain its last two features. Their in-training evaluations are kept in the results folder.

The first attempt used the separate carry path without the zero-initialised scale and with the usual gate bias of $-2$. At step 100, carrying state between slices raised perplexity from 16.3 to 31.5, worse on every source. The reason is the size of a clipped state. The clip bounds the state at $\tau = 20$, which is about 4.2 to 5.3 times the largest singular value of the pretrained down-projection (3.8 to 4.8 in layers 1, 13 and 27). A clipped state concentrated in few directions, as outer-product states tend to be, therefore adds about half the strength of $W_0$ along those directions even through a gate at 12 percent. The zero-initialised scale removes this.

The second attempt added the scale but kept the gate bias at $-2$. This time the in-document fast weights, not the carry, broke the model during early training. With them switched on, perplexity was 4703 at step 50 and 1024 at step 100, against 16.6 and 16.2 with them off, and the training loss opened at 17.5. The carry cannot be the cause, since its scale starts at zero and the failing regime resets the state at every slice, so the spike comes from the Hebbian fast weights within a slice. The model recovered by step 150, but the spike wasted most of a short run. Closing the gate at the start removes it. The second attempt also ran out of GPU memory near step 195, because training through the carry held every slice of a long document in memory, which the window of two slices fixes.

\paragraph{Across documents the carry turns from a small help into a small harm.} With 100 documents, carrying state into a different document of the same source lowers perplexity by 0.010 $[+0.004, +0.018]$ at step 200, mainly on C4 and Wikipedia. By step 400 the effect is gone ($-0.003$), and at step 600 the carried state makes perplexity worse, by 0.020 $[-0.030, -0.013]$, on 71 percent of documents, with a significant harm on four of the six sources. Its gain on the continuation of the same document stays significant throughout, although it is lower at step 600 (0.097) than at step 100 (0.135), while carried into the next document of a chain it becomes interference.

\paragraph{The carry recovers about four percent of the preceding text.} The context suite compares the carry with placing the previous slice directly in attention, scoring the same tokens. At step 600 the previous slice in attention lowers perplexity by 2.770 (25.7 percent), the carried state by 0.110, so the carry recovers 4.0 percent $[3.3, 4.7]$ of that gain, and 3.9 percent $[3.1, 4.6]$ at step 200. It holds 44.0 million values, which in the 32-bit precision used here take about 176 MB, as much as the 16-bit attention cache of roughly 1500 tokens of Qwen3-0.6B (28 layers, 8 key-value heads of dimension 128 under grouped-query attention \citep{ainslie2023gqa}). Its use is therefore where attention cannot reach, not as a cheaper substitute for it.

\paragraph{The carry path stays saturated.} At step 600 the carried state ratio has a median of about 8{,}000 for seed 42 and 15{,}000 for seed 43, and is above the clip threshold on every document by a factor of at least 6400. As for the original models, only the direction of the carried state reaches the output. The redesign works because training through the carry shapes that direction, not because the clip is relieved. Large-chunk TTT and Generative Adapter keep only the direction of their fast weights by design \citep{zhang2025test,chen2024generative}, so a state that reaches the output only as a direction is not unusual for a working fast-weight memory.

\paragraph{Overall, the design has the largest lead over the matched baseline.} The main gate stays nearly closed (median 0.0007 at step 200), so the benefit flows through the carry path. The model leads the matched LoRA baseline by 0.234 $[0.188, 0.293]$ perplexity at step 600 on every document, against 0.106 for the original Hebbian model and 0.203 $[0.165, 0.249]$ for the delta rule at the same step. The intervals of the design and the delta rule overlap, and no paired test between them was run. As for the original models, part of this lead is extra trainable capacity. The design's training gain is 1.971 against 1.859 for the control, so about 0.11 of its lead comes from the slow weights.

\section{A relatedness gradient}

The comparison so far has two points, the same document and a different document of the same source. To test documents that are distinct but related, a further suite uses English Wikipedia, a source on which the redesigned carry gains strongly between slices (Chapter~\ref{ch-q2}). The articles come from two shards of the November 2023 English dump. They may overlap the Wikipedia portion of the training data, which affects every arm alike and so leaves the differences between arms unaffected. For each of 200 target articles, chosen in a seeded order, the second block of 1024 tokens is scored after the carry is reset and after it is primed by a block of 1024 tokens from one of four texts, ordered by relatedness.
\begin{description}
  \item[Same document.] The target article's own first 1024 tokens.
  \item[Related article.] The article the target mentions most often by title, at least twice, matching whole multi-word titles only.
  \item[Random article.] An article that neither mentions the target nor is mentioned by it.
  \item[Other source.] A held-out document from one of the other five SlimPajama sources.
\end{description}
Every prime has the same length and every arm scores exactly the same target tokens, so only the prime varies. Because relatedness is defined by title mentions, a related prime also tends to share more words with the target, and no control here separates relatedness in topic from word overlap. The same 200 cases, identical title for title, were measured on the carry design and on the original Hebbian model, both at step 600.

\section{Comparison with dynamic evaluation}
\label{sec-dyneval}

Dynamic evaluation \citep{krause2018dynamic} is the established way to adapt a model to the text it has just read. The model takes gradient steps on the text it has just read and reads the next text with the adapted weights. As a baseline, the LoRA-only model at step 600 takes Adam steps on its 10.1 million LoRA parameters over each prime, reads the target, and has its weights restored exactly before the next case, with the update made on 32-bit copies so that small steps are not lost to 16-bit rounding. Adapting a small subset of parameters is analogous to the sparse dynamic evaluation of \citet{krause2018dynamic}, but it is not their method, which adapts a zero-initialised matrix acting on part of an LSTM's hidden units, with gradients rescaled by training statistics and a decay towards the trained weights. Our baseline uses Adam with no decay.

\paragraph{Tuning.} The step size and number of steps were chosen on 40 further Wikipedia cases, disjoint from the 200 reported, separately for two objectives, the gain on the same document and the gain from a related article. A first, coarser search gave plain gradient descent a same-document gain of at most 0.149 against 0.441 for Adam, although its grid stopped at a step size of 0.01 while the gain was still rising, so the optimum of gradient descent was not bracketed. Adam degraded sharply from a step size of $3 \times 10^{-4}$, so the final grid uses Adam with step sizes from $3 \times 10^{-6}$ to $10^{-4}$ and 3, 10 or 30 steps. For each objective the best settings lie on a ridge of roughly constant step size times number of steps, about $10^{-4}$ for the related arm and $3 \times 10^{-4}$ for the same document, and settings beyond the ridge diverge, so the grid brackets the optimum. Tuned for the same document the best setting is a step size of $3 \times 10^{-5}$ with 10 steps, and tuned for a related article it is $3 \times 10^{-5}$ with 3 steps. The reported cases are identical, title for title, to those of the carry.

\section{Carry helps after related text and harms after text of another source}

The same mechanism, in the same model, helps on the continuation of the same document throughout training, transfers to a distinct but related document more than dynamic evaluation does, and harms when the next text comes from another source or follows a long chain of other documents. The relatedness suite turns this into a graded result. The carry's benefit falls with relatedness, from the same document to a related article to an unrelated article of the same source, and it becomes harm for text from another source. A trained, gated fast-weight carry is therefore a reliable memory of related text, worth up to about one percent in perplexity, that still transfers a little to unrelated text of the same source and interferes with text of another source. This agrees with the swap result of Chapter~\ref{ch-q1}, where for the original Hebbian model a state primed on a different source made perplexity worse. It also implies that a deployed carry needs a way to decide when to apply it, such as resetting on a change of topic, in the way a selective state-space model can learn to reset its state at a boundary \citep{gu2024mamba}.

\section{What this chapter does not establish}

The ablation is measured at step 200 only, while the relatedness and cross-document results come from step 600. The main results replicate across two seeds, but the ablation and the failed attempts rest on one, and the delta rule was not redesigned. Relatedness is measured on Wikipedia only, and through title mentions, which is a coarse proxy. The context comparison gives the carry one previous slice of text, while the carry itself holds a decayed summary of all earlier slices. Dynamic evaluation was run in a sparse form on LoRA weights, not on all weights, and on the LoRA-only model rather than on the carry model with its carry switched off, so the two arms also differ in their slow weights. Relatedness defined by title mentions is confounded with word overlap. The boundary suite, where a chain of same-source documents harms, and the relatedness suite, where a single unrelated same-source article helps, are not reconciled. No baseline keeps the same memory as attention cache, the last tokens of the previous document, although the carried state is about as large as the cache of 1500 tokens.

\chapter{Discussion}
\label{ch-discussion}

\section{Relation to the update-rule literature}

The delta rule's advantage over the Hebbian rule matches the literature closely. \citet{schlag2021linear} and \citet{yang2024deltanet} predict that an additive store saturates through interference and cannot overwrite stale associations, and \citet{wang2026learning} find an error-correcting write better than In-Place TTT's additive one. Here the delta rule gives a within-document gain up to nine times larger, and only the Hebbian model collapses without the clip, which matches the observation of \citet{schlag2021linear} that the additive rule is unstable without normalisation. The gate closing on the Hebbian model echoes \citet{yang2024gla}, where an ungated additive accumulator trains poorly, and the finding of \citet{mao2022finetuning} that whether error correction helps depends on stabilisation. It also agrees with the reading of the delta rule as online regression, whose update scales with the prediction error \citep{yang2024deltanet}, and with the bound on how many associations a Hebbian state can hold \citep{mcdermott2026lifelong}. The saturated clip has a counterpart in this literature. Normalised reads \citep{katharopoulos2020transformers,beck2024xlstm}, decays \citep{yang2024gla,behrouz2025titans} and the bounded transition of the delta rule \citep{yang2024deltanet} keep a state in range as part of the update itself, whereas a clip applied at read time bounds it from outside and keeps only its direction, which is the distinction Chapter~\ref{ch-why} turns on.

\section{Relation to persistence and continual learning}

The null result of the naive carry across documents is consistent with the stability-plasticity dilemma \citep{french1999catastrophic}. The read-time clip buys stability by removing plasticity in the one quantity, the state's magnitude, that a carry needs to accumulate evidence. Successful persistence mechanisms in the literature avoid this in different ways. Transformer-XL carries cached activations within one stream without updating any weight \citep{dai2019transformerxl}, the Recurrent Memory Transformer trains its memory tokens through several segments \citep{bulatov2022rmt}, dynamic evaluation anchors updates to the trained weights with a decay \citep{krause2018dynamic}, and gated and selective state updates let a model choose what to keep \citep{gu2024mamba,yang2025gated}. The naive carry has a fixed decay and no selectivity, and it receives no training signal across documents, since training sessions never cross a document boundary and backpropagation stops at each item. The redesign adds a learned carry gate and training through the boundary between slices, but it, too, is never trained across documents. Continual test-time adaptation in vision shows the same tension, since a model that is never reset accumulates errors and forgets unless part of it is regularly restored \citep{maharana2026continual}, and a test-time cache that keeps more noisy state becomes worse rather than better \citep{karmanov2024efficient}. Sequential fact writing into weights shows the same pattern, where retention of an earlier fact depends mostly on how the later writes are made \citep{oneill2026continually}, as our carry is harmed by a long chain of later documents. The naive carry's null result also sits beside the finding of \citet{ouyang2026ttt} that, under matched training, the original In-Place TTT target does not improve RULER over the backbone, which suggests that small gains from this layer depend heavily on what it writes.

\section{Related versus unrelated text}

The redesigned carry separates two questions that the original design left entangled. Whether a carried fast weight can hold useful information is answered yes, since it helps on the continuation of the same document once it is read through its own path and trained through. Whether it transfers beyond that document is answered by the relatedness gradient. It transfers to a related article, more than dynamic evaluation does, less to an unrelated article of the same source, and it harms after text from another source. The contrast with dynamic evaluation suggests why. Gradient steps fit the text they were taken on, while a carry that was trained to be read by a later slice learns to hold what a later text can use. The update-rule and continual-learning literature anticipates both halves. Gated and selective memories, which decide what to keep \citep{gu2024mamba,yang2025gated}, and persistence mechanisms trained through the boundary \citep{bulatov2022rmt}, are the ingredients the redesign adds, and the interference between new and old associations in shared weights that \citet{french1999catastrophic} describes is a natural account of the harm after text from another source.

The graded result also has precedents in test-time training. \citet{sun2020ttt} show, in the convex case, that a test-time update helps when its gradient is aligned with that of the main task, \citet{gandelsman2022ttt} find that a misaligned self-supervised signal lowers accuracy, and \citet{akyurek2025surprising} find that whether an adapter shared across tasks helps or interferes depends on the tasks. One reading of the relatedness gradient in these terms is that a carry built from related text acts as an update aligned with the target, and one built from another source as a misaligned one. Two further results fit the literature. That the carry does not accumulate with repeated reading resembles the observation of \citet{krause2018dynamic} that the benefit of dynamic evaluation levels off within a few thousand characters of in-distribution text. And the far larger gain from placing the preceding text in attention matches the report that full attention retrieves much better than test-time training \citep{tandon2025endtoend}, and the caution of the RWKV authors that a fixed-size recurrent state may limit the recall of fine detail \citep{peng2023rwkv}, and that attention, not a persistent update, explains easier second occurrences of new words while they are still in the context \citep{lazaridou2021mind}. Generative Adapter shows the same ordering for a weight update built in a forward pass, which reaches an F1 of 40.2 on multi-session conversations against 66.0 for prompting with the full history \citep{chen2024generative}.

\section{Implications}

For practitioners, the results suggest that a carried fast weight is worth keeping across documents only when the next document is related, and that it should be reset when the topic or source changes, while within a document attention remains far stronger. For research on session-persistent test-time learning, they suggest that the write of a carried state has to be trained for a later read, as the redesign does by training through the carry, and that this can work even when a read-time clip discards the state's magnitude. A bound that is part of the state's design, for example a normalised or gated memory that keeps its magnitude in a usable range \citep{beck2024xlstm,yang2025gated}, might let the magnitude carry information as well, and a training signal that spans document boundaries is the natural next step.

\chapter{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{ch-limits}

\paragraph{Capacity confound.} The TTT models train about 6.7 times as many parameters as the control. Most of their lead over the control is this extra capacity, and a control with matched parameter count, such as the same architecture trained with fast-weight updates disabled, would separate the two cleanly. It was not run for budget reasons. The within-document and between-slice gains are the measures that isolate the mechanism. The same confound applies to the redesign, whose training gain exceeds the control's by about 0.11 of its 0.234 lead.

\paragraph{Carry trained within documents only.} Every training session is one document, so the models learn to use a carried state between slices of a document but are never trained with a state carried from a different document. The cross-document result therefore measures whether a within-document carry transfers across documents, not what a model trained on multi-document sessions would learn. The clip analysis in Chapter~\ref{ch-why} applies to both cases, since the clip saturates on every measurement regardless of how the state was built.

\paragraph{Scale.} One model of 0.6 billion parameters is studied, against the 0.6 to 4 billion range planned in the proposal, with one seed per configuration except the carry design, which has two, and at most 600 of 1050 optimiser steps. The null result of the naive carry is stable across 200, 400 and 600 steps, which argues against undertraining within this budget, but larger models and longer training are untested. Forgetting under sequential fine-tuning has been found to grow with model size \citep{li2024revisiting}, so the balance between transfer and interference may shift at larger scale.

\paragraph{Evaluation set.} The held-out set has 106 documents, with only 6 from Book. For the original models the cross-document test rests on 24 documents, about four per source, so it can rule out only effects larger than about 0.01. The redesign's boundary suite uses 100.

\paragraph{No-clip probes.} The runs without the clip are short, 100 to 200 steps, and single-seed. They show that removing the clip does not rescue persistence early in training, not what a long unclipped run would converge to.

\paragraph{Carry design.} The redesigned carry of Chapter~\ref{ch-design} is replicated with two seeds, which is enough to show its results are not a fluke but not to report a spread, its ablation stops at step 200 with one seed, and it exists for the Hebbian rule only. Its relatedness gradient is measured on Wikipedia only, with relatedness defined by title mentions, and the comparison with dynamic evaluation uses a sparse form on the LoRA weights of a different model, the LoRA-only control. There is no baseline that keeps an equal amount of memory as attention cache, and the gap between the boundary and relatedness suites is unexplained.

\paragraph{Perplexity only.} All evidence is perplexity-based, and perplexity alone does not show how generated text or downstream behaviour changes \citep{meister2021beyond}. Lower loss can coexist with no behavioural recall \citep{song2026beyond}, and a written fact can leave a raised log-probability after the model stops using it \citep{oneill2026continually}, so our results support claims about stream and domain adaptation, not about memory. A diagnostic that withholds the earlier text, in the spirit of LongBench \citep{bai2023longbench}, would help separate what is carried from what the base model already knows. The RULER probes planned in the proposal were not run, and no behavioural test of retained information was attempted, consistent with the proposal's scope. Whether the carry helps where attention cannot reach, for example after the previous document has left the context, is therefore argued from memory size rather than tested.

\paragraph{Future work.} For the carry design, the most direct extensions are further seeds to report a spread, the relatedness gradient on other domains and with finer measures of relatedness, a mechanism that decides when to apply the carry, and the same design for the delta rule. The most important missing comparisons are a baseline that keeps the same memory as an attention cache of the previous document's last tokens, dynamic evaluation of the carry model itself with its carry switched off, a control for word overlap in the relatedness test, and a behavioural probe after the earlier text has left the context. More broadly, the extensions are a parameter-matched control, dynamic evaluation on all weights, a carried state with a designed bound such as a normalised or gated memory, a learned rather than fixed decay of the carried state \citep{ravi2017optimization,yang2024gla}, training through document boundaries so that the carry receives a learning signal, and replication at larger scale with several seeds.

\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch-conclusion}

This thesis asked whether the fast weights that test-time training builds while reading one document can be carried into the next. They can, if the carry is designed and trained for it, and what they carry is specific. A trained, gated carry transfers across documents according to their relatedness. Its benefit falls steadily from a related article to an unrelated one of the same source and turns to harm for text from another source and after a long chain of other documents, it transfers more than dynamic evaluation tuned for the same purpose while needing only forward passes, and it holds across two training seeds. It is a memory of recent related text and not of everything read, since repeated reading adds nothing beyond the first, and next to attention on the preceding text it recovers only about four percent of the gain, so its place is where attention cannot reach.

Reaching this design required understanding why the naive carry fails. The norm clip that keeps the fast weights stable is saturated on every measurement, so a carried state reaches the output only as a direction, and in short probes removing the clip made the model either collapse or switch the fast weights off. Giving the carry its own zero-initialised path and training it through the boundary between slices, two changes that in a single-seed ablation appear to work together, makes that direction useful. It raises the Hebbian carry between slices from at most 0.014 to about 0.1 and gives the largest lead over the matched baseline in the study, with the clip still saturated. Along the way the attribution shows that most of a TTT model's lead over a matched baseline is extra trainable capacity, and that within a document the delta rule adapts several times more than the Hebbian rule. The open questions are whether the gradient holds beyond Wikipedia and at larger scale, and how a model should decide when to apply its carry, since applying it to unrelated text does harm.
